## Fragility Case


### Procurement Strategy Under Chokepoint Instability


### Peter Bell

Version date: March 23, 2026 ORCID: 0009-0004-0415-7774 DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.19195048 Abstract This article analyzes the Strait of Hormuz as a marine supply-chain fragility case rather than only as a geopolitical flashpoint. Its central argument is that procurement fragility appears before total closure:
once a high-volume chokepoint becomes insecure, operators alter behavior through delay, avoidance, repricing, and contractual caution even when passage remains physically open. The paper shows that Hormuz combines extreme route concentration, limited bypass capacity, live security threats, and cross- market spillovers into a single high-consequence operating environment. It then traces how these conditions propagate into freight, insurance, bunkering, scheduling, supplier reliability, and contract performance. The article argues that marine procurement strategy under chokepoint instability must shift from cost optimization toward resilience optimization through contractual flexibility, dependable counterparties, compliant contingency planning, and continuous risk monitoring. Hormuz is presented as a model case for understanding how concentrated maritime dependence becomes operational fragility.
(EIA 2026; UNCTAD 2026; IMO 2026; INTERTANKO 2026; BIMCO 2026)

#### 1. Introduction

The Strait of Hormuz is one of the most consequential maritime chokepoints in the world economy.
Large volumes of oil and liquefied natural gas move through a narrow passage whose disruption would affect not only regional shipping but also globally distributed energy, transport, and industrial systems.
For that reason, Hormuz is more than a regional security issue. It is a concentrated transport dependency with broad commercial consequences. (EIA 2026) For marine procurement and supplier-management analysis, however, the key issue is not only whether the strait closes completely. The more useful question is how operators behave when a narrow, high-volume corridor becomes insecure. The central claim of this article is that procurement fragility appears before closure. Once threat and uncertainty begin to alter transit timing, insurance pricing, routing decisions, and supplier confidence, procurement strategy can no longer be organized primarily around lowest nominal cost. It must instead shift toward resilience. (IMO 2026; INTERTANKO 2026; BIMCO 2026) This article therefore examines the Strait of Hormuz as a marine supply-chain fragility mechanism. It argues that the case is analytically important because concentrated route dependence, limited bypass capacity, live security risk, and constrained substitution combine to create disproportionate downstream effects. The paper proceeds from chokepoint definition to dependence structure, then to threat mechanisms, procurement consequences, response levers, compliance constraints, and a broader fragility framework. (EIA 2026; UNCTAD 2026)

#### 2. The Strait of Hormuz as a Marine Chokepoint

The Strait of Hormuz connects the Persian Gulf to the Gulf of Oman and the Arabian Sea through a narrow and operationally constrained passage. Its importance lies not simply in geography, but in the combination of narrow routing, dense traffic, and limited alternative pathways. Hormuz is therefore best understood as a chokepoint in the strict sense: a passage whose constrained geometry magnifies the consequences of disruption. (EIA 2026) This structural importance is reinforced by incomplete bypass capacity. Alternative pipeline routes in the region can offset only part of a major disruption, not replace the full flow that normally transits the strait. In other words, the route is not easily substitutable. That matters because chokepoint vulnerability is not defined only by danger, but by the limited availability of credible alternatives once danger escalates. (EIA 2026) For this paper, Hormuz is treated as a marine chokepoint whose narrow operating environment and incomplete substitution capacity make it a high-consequence case for supply-chain analysis. The point of the section is therefore definitional: Hormuz is structurally important before any particular crisis is considered.

#### 3. Supply-Chain Dependence Structure

What depends on Hormuz is broader than a simple oil-route description suggests. Large crude oil, condensate, petroleum-product, and LNG flows move through the strait, and destination dependence is heavily concentrated in Asian markets. This means that the case is not merely about one commodity stream, but about a layered transport corridor linking producers, carriers, receivers, and downstream industrial users. (EIA 2026) The dependence structure also extends beyond cargo ownership. Tanker operators, charterers, bunker buyers, marine service providers, agents, and receiving facilities all become exposed to the same passage risk. When the route becomes uncertain, the effect is not confined to commodity throughput. It propagates into vessel scheduling, port timing, fuel planning, maintenance coordination, and supplier commitments.
The dependence surface is therefore multi-layered. Cargo flows depend on transit, vessel operations depend on timing, and procurement commitments depend on both. This is why Hormuz matters for marine procurement specifically: it concentrates multiple operational dependencies into one passage, making failure modes correlated rather than isolated. (EIA 2026; UNCTAD 2026)

#### 4. Threat Mechanisms and Disruption Pathways

The threat environment in Hormuz should be understood as a set of interacting commercial-shipping risks rather than a single closure scenario. Illegal boarding, detention, and seizure threaten the continuity of voyages directly. UAV attacks, mine-related risks, and other forms of vessel targeting create obvious physical disruption risks. These mechanisms can damage ships, endanger crews, interrupt cargo movement, and trigger immediate insurance and legal consequences. (MARAD 2025; MARAD 2023) But direct physical attack is only one part of the mechanism set. A second mechanism is uncertainty amplification. Once attack risk, navigation interference, or inconsistent operating information rises, operators may delay transit, slow movement, avoid the area where possible, or require additional internal approval before proceeding. The practical result is that insecurity changes shipping behavior even when the route remains technically open. (IMO 2026; INTERTANKO 2026)

Partial threat, degraded information, and cautionary guidance are enough to alter timing, routing, and vessel availability. Hormuz fragility therefore operates through both physical interruption and expectation-driven behavior change.

#### 5. Procurement Consequences of Chokepoint Instability

Once these threat mechanisms are translated into commercial terms, the procurement consequences become clearer. Insurance repricing, freight volatility, delayed transits, longer voyages, and more cautious vessel behavior all change the assumptions on which procurement decisions are normally made. In practical terms, the problem begins when passage becomes less reliable and more expensive, not only when passage stops altogether. (UNCTAD 2026; BIMCO 2026) This change in expected reliability affects several layers at once. Voyage economics become harder to predict. Bunkering plans become more volatile. Delivery windows become less credible. Supplier performance becomes more difficult to enforce under routine assumptions. Contracts that looked efficient under stable conditions become stressed once delay, deviation, or altered insurance costs appear. (BIMCO 2026; BIMCO 2025a; BIMCO 2025b) The key inference is that procurement disruption begins before closure. Once instability changes the expected cost and reliability of transit, a lowest-price procurement model becomes increasingly inadequate. Marine procurement must then be read as a continuity problem rather than a simple purchasing problem.

#### 6. Supplier and Contract Response Levers

The response layer begins before a ship enters the strait. Operationally, managers may need to defer transit, alter timing, strengthen voyage-specific risk assessment, maintain closer liaison with maritime security channels, and prepare crews and systems for degraded-information conditions. These are not abstract security measures. They are procurement-relevant because they alter when services can be delivered, how reliably suppliers can perform, and what assumptions can still be used in scheduling.
(INTERTANKO 2026) A second response layer is contractual. Under chokepoint instability, war-risk and force-majeure clauses stop being boilerplate and become operating tools. Delay, deviation, alternative orders, notice obligations, mitigation duties, and additional insurance costs all become commercially active. The practical value of a contract under these conditions is not simply whether it allocates blame correctly, but whether it preserves workable options under stress. (BIMCO 2026; BIMCO 2025a; BIMCO 2025b; BIMCO 2022) A third response layer is relational. Resilient suppliers are not merely low-cost suppliers. They are counterparties capable of communication, coordination, and alternative performance when routine execution fails. The strategic implication is that response instruments should be evaluated by how much continuity they preserve, not only by how much nominal cost they save in stable periods.

#### 7. Compliance and Operating Constraints

Crisis does not suspend maritime compliance. Security obligations under SOLAS and the ISPS framework remain active, meaning that ships and companies continue to operate within formal security planning and reporting structures. Likewise, navigation obligations and carriage requirements remain in

(IMO n.d.a; IMO n.d.b) Environmental and fuel-compliance duties also remain active. Rerouting, delayed bunkering, or contingency fuel sourcing still have to occur within applicable sulfur-cap and documentation requirements. This matters because it prevents the paper from implying unconstrained operational freedom. A response that is commercially attractive but non-compliant is not a real resilience option.
(IMO n.d.c) The implication for procurement strategy is that resilience must be built inside the compliance envelope. Planning for disruption is not only about alternative performance, but about alternative performance that remains legally, operationally, and environmentally executable.

#### 8. From Cost Efficiency to Resilience: A Fragility Framework

The Hormuz case supports a procurement-centered definition of fragility. Structurally, fragility begins when high-volume dependence is concentrated in a narrow route with limited bypass capacity.
Operationally, fragility intensifies when insecurity below the threshold of closure changes behavior through delay, caution, avoidance, and degraded-information navigation. Commercially, fragility becomes visible when those changes propagate into insurance, freight, bunkering, supplier reliability, and contract performance. (EIA 2026; IMO 2026; INTERTANKO 2026; BIMCO 2026) This argument can be stated in compact schematic form. Let D denote route dependence, T threat intensity or operational uncertainty, S substitution capacity, and R procurement resilience capacity.
Procurement fragility can then be represented as F = (D × T) / (S + R) where F is fragility in the procurement environment. On this view, fragility rises when dependence and insecurity increase, and falls when substitution options or resilience capacity increase.
The central implication is that a chokepoint need not be physically shut to become operationally disruptive. Fragility emerges earlier, at the point where insecurity is sufficient to alter expected reliability, cost, routing behavior, insurance conditions, and contract performance. In the Hormuz case, this means that procurement instability begins when operators no longer trust passage conditions to remain commercially routine, even if vessels can still pass through the strait.

Page 5 In this sense, fragility is not equivalent to collapse. It is the condition in which disruption and uncertainty generate consequences disproportionate to the initiating event because dependence is concentrated and substitution is constrained. Hormuz demonstrates this clearly: limited incidents or elevated threat levels can produce wider downstream consequences even without complete interruption of passage. (UNCTAD 2026) Resilience, by contrast, is not the absence of exposure. It is the capacity to preserve feasible performance under conditions of concentrated dependence and rising uncertainty. The relevant shift is therefore from cost efficiency under normal conditions to resilience under instability.

#### 9. Practical Strategy for Marine Procurement Managers

For marine procurement managers, the first priority is pre-crisis preparation. This includes reviewing war-risk and force-majeure clauses, identifying dependable counterparties, planning alternative service and bunkering options, and clarifying how compliance obligations will be met if routing or timing changes. Resilience is strongest when these arrangements exist before escalation begins. (BIMCO 2026; BIMCO 2022; IMO n.d.c) The second priority is continuous monitoring. In a Hormuz-type environment, managers need to watch not only formal security advisories, but also war-risk pricing, freight behavior, navigation interference, vessel movement patterns, and the practical viability of alternative timing or routing.
Conditions that still look commercially tolerable on paper may already be degrading in operational reality. (INTERTANKO 2026; IMO 2026) The third priority, once instability escalates, is to shift decision logic. At that stage, the relevant question is no longer which option is cheapest under ideal assumptions. It is which option most credibly preserves continuity while remaining contractually workable and compliant. This means giving more weight to dependable counterparties, flexible arrangements, and executable alternatives than to marginal savings under unstable conditions.

#### 10. Conclusion

The Strait of Hormuz is best understood here not merely as a geopolitical flashpoint, but as a marine supply-chain fragility mechanism. Its significance lies in the combination of concentrated dependence, limited substitution, live security threat, and the ability of partial disruption to generate disproportionate downstream consequences. (EIA 2026; UNCTAD 2026) The article’s central conclusion is therefore narrower and stronger than the generic claim that closure would be catastrophic. The more useful insight is that procurement fragility appears before closure. Once threat and uncertainty begin to reprice passage and weaken transit reliability, procurement logic changes.
Marine procurement strategy must then shift from cost optimization toward resilience optimization through contractual flexibility, dependable counterparties, compliant contingency planning, and disciplined monitoring. (BIMCO 2026; INTERTANKO 2026; IMO n.d.c) In that sense, Hormuz functions as a model case for understanding how concentrated maritime dependence becomes operational fragility. The broader contribution of the paper is to show that chokepoint analysis becomes more useful when it is translated from geopolitical description into procurement and supply-chain consequence.
